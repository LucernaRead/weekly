THE BIG STORY · Schlagzeile

Democrats Win the House and the Senate

By The Lucerna newsroom · Reading level: A2

On Tuesday, November 3, Americans voted in the midterm election — for all 435 seats in the House of Representatives, for 35 seats in the Senate, and for governor in 36 states. By Sunday, November 8, the count was clear enough to call: the Democrats won the House, and the Democrats won the Senate.

The numbers, as called by the Associated Press on Sunday, November 8: in the House, 254 seats for the Democrats and 162 for the Republicans, with 19 races still uncalled. The majority is 218. In the Senate, the Democrats won 20 of the 33 races called, and the Republicans 13, with two races — in Alaska and Mississippi — still uncalled.

Before the vote, the Republicans held the House, 218 seats to 214, and the Senate, 53 to 47. On election night, seven Senate seats changed hands — in Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas — and all seven went to the Democrats. Five governors' offices changed hands too: Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio and Texas.

A “call” is not the official result. News organisations call a race when the maths is clear. The official result comes later, when each state checks its count and certifies it — between November 10 and December 18. Until then, the numbers can still move a little. Page 6 explains how to read them.

The new Congress meets in January 2027. Until then, the old Congress finishes its work — and the winners plan theirs. This week also brings the jungle back to television, the Ashes to Perth, and a new Hunger Games film to the cinema. Welcome back to VOL.4!

The Week After the Vote: What Happens Now?

By The Lucerna newsroom · Reading level: A2

The vote is over. The counting is not. On Monday, November 9, the day this issue went to press, election workers in many states were still opening mail ballots and checking signatures. In Alaska and Mississippi, the Senate races were still too close to call.

What happens next? First, the states certify their counts — between November 10 and December 18. If no candidate wins a majority in Georgia's Senate race, there is a runoff on December 1. Mississippi has no runoff: the most votes win. Then the winners are sworn in, and the new Congress meets in January 2027.

In Washington, the parties choose their leaders. The Republicans in the House vote on Tuesday, November 10. The Democrats in the House vote on November 18 and 19. The Speaker — the leader of the House — is chosen when the new Congress meets in January. The Speaker, Mike Johnson, has said he will not run to lead the Republicans in the new Congress.

TWO HOUSES, TWO JOBS

Why two houses? The House of Representatives has 435 seats, and every seat is on the ballot every two years. The Senate has 100 seats, and about a third of them are on the ballot each time. A senator serves for six years. A member of the House serves for two. That is why the House changes fast, and the Senate changes slowly.

THE SENATE, IN NUMBERS · Zahlen

100 — seats in the Senate. 35 — seats on the ballot on November 3. 6 — years in a senator's term.

Life goes on around the politics. Next week is Thanksgiving week in America. Thanksgiving Day is Thursday, November 26 — a day for family, for a big dinner, and for the turkey. Many families travel home, and the Friday after it, November 27, is Black Friday, the biggest shopping day of the year.

And in Britain? The week is quieter — but the television is not. On Monday, November 16, at 9 p.m., the jungle opens again: I'm a Celebrity is back on ITV1. Page 3 has the story — and the words you need for the jungle.

Saturday in Perth: The Ashes Are Back

By The Lucerna newsroom · Reading level: B1

On Saturday, November 21, the oldest rivalry in cricket returns. Australia play England in the first Test of the Ashes, at Perth Stadium. The first ball is at 10:20 a.m. local time — 2:20 a.m. in London. Day one is already sold out.

Australia have problems. Their captain, Pat Cummins, is out with a back injury, and the fast bowler Josh Hazlewood is out with a hamstring injury. Steve Smith leads the side instead, and Scott Boland takes Cummins's place. The young fast bowler Brendan Doggett may play his first Test.

England's team is more settled. Ben Stokes is the captain. Joe Root, the batter England need most, is in the team, with Jofra Archer and Mark Wood to bowl fast. England have not won a Test series in Australia since 2010–11 — they lost 4–0 on their last two tours, in 2017–18 and 2021–22. The last series, in England in 2023, was drawn.

One Test lasts up to five days: November 21 to 25. There are five Tests in all. The Ashes are not a sport for early nights.

Back home, football returns too. The Premier League is back on Saturday, November 21, after the international break: Manchester City play Fulham at 12:30, and Newcastle play Arsenal at 5:30. On Sunday, November 22, Liverpool play Manchester United at 4:30 — and the ATP Finals end in Turin, with the singles final not before 6 p.m. local time.

WHY PERTH? · Warum Perth?

Perth Stadium has never hosted an Ashes Test. England's last tour skipped Perth — travel to Western Australia was shut by the pandemic. Australia lost their first Test at the ground in November 2024, by 295 runs to India.

How do you follow three sports in one day? Choose one game to watch fully, and follow the others on your phone. That is the modern fan: one screen for the game, one screen for the scores.

The ATP Finals in Turin run from Sunday, November 15, to Sunday, November 22. Break the Silence: Odd Socks and Loud Voices

By The Lucerna newsroom · Reading level: B1

On Monday, November 16, schools across Britain do something strange: children and teachers wear odd socks. One red sock, one blue sock — on purpose. It is Odd Socks Day, the first day of Anti-Bullying Week, and this year is its tenth birthday.

Anti-Bullying Week runs from Monday, November 16, to Friday, November 20. It is organised by the Anti-Bullying Alliance, and this year's theme is “Break the Silence”. The idea is simple: bullying is hardest to stop when nobody speaks about it. So the week asks children — and adults — to start conversations, to speak up, to listen, and to take action together.

The numbers are big: the campaign reaches about eight million children and young people, and more than 80% of schools in England, Wales and Northern Ireland take part. The theme was chosen with more than 200 children and young people, who said that speaking up was the hardest part.

Friday, November 20, is also World Children's Day — a day for children's rights around the world, led by UNICEF. This year's theme is “My day. My rights. My future.” The date marks the day in 1989 when the United Nations adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child.

What can one child do? Tell a teacher. Tell a parent. Tell a friend. And if you see someone alone at lunch, sit with them. A small act is not small to the person it helps — and a loud voice starts with one sentence.

ODD SOCKS, IN NUMBERS · Zahlen

10 — years of Odd Socks Day. 8 million — children reached by the week. 200 — children who helped choose this year's theme.

If you want to practise Monday-morning English, try this: wear odd socks, and explain them in English. “I wear odd socks for Anti-Bullying Week. Different is good.” Three sentences — and a whole week of practice.

The Map After November 3: Reading the Results

By The Lucerna newsroom · Reading level: B2

The vote is counted. The map is still being drawn. On Tuesday, November 3, Americans voted for the House, for a third of the Senate, and for governor in 36 states. By the Sunday after the vote, November 8, the Associated Press had called the big question: the Democrats had won both houses of Congress.

Here is the map, as called on Sunday, November 8. In the House, the Democrats won 254 seats, the Republicans 162, and 19 races were still uncalled. In the Senate, the Democrats won 20 of the 33 races called and the Republicans 13, with Alaska and Mississippi still uncalled. Seven Senate seats changed hands, and all seven went to the Democrats: Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas.

What does “called” mean? A call is a news organisation's judgement: the maths is clear, and the candidate behind cannot catch up. It is not the official result. The official result comes when each state certifies its count — between November 10 and December 18, with no fixed date in some states. In Georgia, a Senate race with no majority winner goes to a runoff on December 1. Mississippi has no runoff in a regular election: the candidate with the most votes wins.

What changes? In January 2027, the new Congress meets, with a Democratic House and a Democratic Senate. The two parties choose their leaders first: the House Republicans on November 10, the House Democrats on November 18 and 19. The Speaker — the leader of the House — is chosen when the new Congress meets in January. The Speaker, Mike Johnson, has said he will not run to lead the Republicans in the new Congress.

What does not change? The President stays the same, and so does the White House. A midterm changes Congress, not the President. That is the American system: the voters speak in the middle of a President's term, and the two sides must then work — or argue — together. The map is drawn. The work starts in January.

WORDS · A2 · win / won

Erklärung | Win ist Gegenwart; won ist Vergangenheit (hat schon gewonnen) — in dieser Woche steht meist die Vergangenheit.

win = to get first place. won = the past of win.

A2: “The Democrats won the House.” The vote is over, so the verb is in the past: won, not win.

Try it: win / won — ask “Will England win the Ashes?” before the series starts. After the first Test: “England won the first Test.”

WORDS · B1 · more than

Erklärung | comparative = Komparativ: mit more than spricht man über Menge und Größe.

more than = a bigger number than. In the House, 254 is more than 218. The majority is 218 — and 254 is 36 more than the majority.

B1: “254 seats is more than the 218 needed.” With numbers, use more than: more people, more seats, more votes.

Try it: “The film is longer than the trailer.” “The book is cheaper than the film.”

GRAMMAR · A2 · will + verb

Erklärung | will + Grundform: für das, was geschehen wird; Fragen mit will am Anfang.

The counting will continue. The new Congress will meet in January. Will the count change? Nobody knows.

Rule: will + verb, no -s, no -ed. “She will win.” “They will vote.” Question: “Will they watch the jungle?”

GRAMMAR · B1 · past simple

Erklärung | past simple = einfache Vergangenheit; unregelmäßige Verben muss man lernen.

Use the past for the past: Collins published the novel in 2025. Haymitch won the 50th Games. England lost 4–0 in 2021–22. India beat Australia by 295 runs.

Irregular verbs in this issue: win → won, lose → lost, beat → beat, choose → chose, run → ran.

GRAMMAR · B2 · headlines

Erklärung | headline present = Präsens in Schlagzeilen: Zeitungen schreiben im Präsens über Geschehenes.

Newspapers use the present in headlines for what already happened: “Democrats Win the House and the Senate” — the vote was on November 3, but the headline says Win, not Won. The present makes the news feel fresh.

GRAMMAR · B2 · whether

Erklärung | whether leitet einen Nebensatz ein: für das, was man nicht weiß.

Nobody knows whether the count will change. Nobody knows whether it will rain in Perth. Whether = if — for what you do not know.
