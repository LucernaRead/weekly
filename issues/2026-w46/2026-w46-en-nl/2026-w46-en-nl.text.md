Eleven Eleven : The Two Minutes When the English-Speaking World Stops

On Wednesday, November 11, at eleven o'clock in the morning, something quiet happens across the English-speaking world. In London, Ottawa, Canberra and Washington, people stop what they are doing, and for two minutes they stand still and say nothing. It is the two-minute silence — for the soldiers who died in war, and for all who served.

The date is not an accident. At 11 a.m. on November 11, 1918, the guns of the First World War fell silent. The armistice — the agreement to stop fighting — began at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. In Britain it is Remembrance Day ; in the United States, Veterans Day, for all who served.

The symbol is the poppy, a small red flower. Poppies grew on the battlefields of Flanders, where so many soldiers died. People wear a paper poppy in November, to help veterans and their families. In London, the King leads the National Act of Remembrance at the Cenotaph in Whitehall — the monument to the dead with no grave of their own.

This week is full of other dates too. On Friday, November 13, the BBC's Children in Need appeal fills British television, and a new Scrooge comes to the cinema. On Sunday, November 15, American football comes to Munich, tennis opens its season finale in Turin, and England play Spain in Madrid.

And one promise from last issue : the midterm election results. This issue went to press on November 2, before the November 3 vote — so it prints no results, and invents none. Page 6 explains why counting takes time. Welcome back to VOL.3 !

LIFE　Leven

LEVEL A2　Silence, poppies & your week　De stilte, de klaprozen en jouw week

On Wednesday, November 11, at 11 a.m., Britain stops. Buses stop. Shops go quiet. In schools, children stand still. For two minutes, nobody speaks. The silence remembers the soldiers who died in the First World War — and in every war since.

Why eleven eleven ? The First World War ended at 11 a.m. on November 11, 1918. The armistice was signed that morning, and the guns stopped. In 1920, Britain built the Cenotaph in Whitehall, London — a stone monument with no body inside it, for all the dead. Every year since, people have gathered there.

In Canada, Australia and New Zealand, the day is the same : Remembrance Day, the same poppy, the same silence at 11 a.m. In the United States, November 11 is Veterans Day — a day for all veterans, living and dead. The names are different. The silence is the same.

The poppy is small, red and paper. Volunteers sell it on streets and in shops, and the money helps veterans and their families. Many people wear it on the left, near the heart — and they wear it all through November, until the day itself is past.

And what about the weekend just gone ? On Sunday, November 8, it was Remembrance Sunday in Britain — the main ceremony, three days before Armistice Day itself. And in India and in Indian homes around the world, Sunday, November 8, was Diwali, the festival of lights : a five-day festival of lamps, sweets and family, with the main night on Sunday. Two festivals of light and memory, one weekend — both just passed, not coming.

This week looks forward too. On Wednesday, November 11, shoppers mark Singles' Day — 11.11, the biggest online shopping day in China, now copied around the world. On Friday, November 13, Britain's Children in Need appeal takes over television. Page 5 has the story of Pudsey the bear.

DEEP READ　Verdieping

LEVEL B2　After November 3　Na 3 november

On Tuesday, November 3, Americans voted in the midterm election — all 435 seats in the House of Representatives, about a third of the Senate, and governorships in many states. This issue went to press on Monday, November 2, the day before the vote. That is why it prints no results. There were no results to print — and this paper invents none.

Some races are called on election night. A news organisation « calls » a race when the maths is clear : the candidate behind cannot catch up. But a call is not the official result. The official result comes later, when each state checks — the canvass — and certifies its count. States certify between November 10 and December 18, and in some states there is no fixed date at all.

Why does counting take days ? Mail ballots take time : every envelope must be opened, every signature checked. Some states count mail ballots that arrive after Election Day, if they were posted in time. In Alaska and Maine, ranked-choice voting can add rounds — and days. In Georgia and Mississippi, a Senate race with no majority winner goes to a runoff on December 1.

Leads can change as the count goes on. In some states, the votes cast on the day are counted first ; in others, mail votes come first. A candidate who leads at midnight can trail by Friday — and that is normal. It is not a sign of anything wrong. It is the system, working slowly, in public.

The last midterm, in 2022, tells the same story. The Senate was not decided until the Saturday after Election Day, and the House until the Wednesday after. Nobody knows whether this year will be faster or slower — and this paper will not guess.

ELEVEN ELEVEN

« At eleven o'clock, we stop. » Eleven is the number of the week : the eleventh hour, the eleventh day, the eleventh month. Say it slowly : e-LEV-en. The silence starts at eleven — and the word is easy to remember, because the date is the word.

For a plan or a promise : « We will remember them. » will + the verb, with no change. It is the future of the week — use it for every promise of November 11.
