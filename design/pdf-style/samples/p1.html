The Big Story 头条

Eleven Eleven: The Two Minutes When the English-Speaking World Stops

By The Lucerna newsroomReading level A2

On Wednesday, November 11, at eleven o'clock in the morning, something quiet happens across the English-speaking world. In London, Ottawa, Canberra and Washington, people stop what they are doing, and for two minutes they stand still and say nothing. It is the two-minute silence — for the soldiers who died in war, and for all who served.

The date is not an accident. At 11 a.m. on November 11, 1918, the guns of the First World War fell silent. The armistice — the agreement to stop fighting — began at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. In Britain it is Remembrance Day; in the United States, Veterans Day, for all who served.

The symbol is the poppy, a small red flower. Poppies grew on the battlefields of Flanders, where so many soldiers died. People wear a paper poppy in November, to help veterans and their families. In London, the King leads the National Act of Remembrance at the Cenotaph in Whitehall — the monument to the dead with no grave of their own.

This week is full of other dates too. On Friday, November 13, the BBC's Children in Need appeal fills British television, and a new Scrooge comes to the cinema. On Sunday, November 15, American football comes to Munich, tennis opens its season finale in Turin, and England play Spain in Madrid.

And one promise from last issue: the midterm election results. This issue went to press on November 2, before the November 3 vote — so it prints no results, and invents none. Page 6 explains why counting takes time. Welcome back to VOL.3!

Four Countries, One Minute

One silence, four names

In the United States it is Veterans Day. In Britain it is Remembrance Day. In Canada and Australia, the name is the same too — with the same poppy and the same 11 a.m. silence.
